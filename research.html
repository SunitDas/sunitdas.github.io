Research Overview

I am a theoretical condensed matter physicist working on quantum geometry, the structure encoded in how Bloch wavefunctions evolve across the Brillouin zone, and on the transport and optical phenomena it generates in real materials.

The organizing idea behind my work is simple to state and surprisingly far-reaching. A band's energy dispersion alone does not determine how a material responds to fields. The geometry of its wavefunctions, expressed through the Berry curvature, the quantum metric, orbital and spin moments, and their mixed layer- and spin-resolved generalizations, contributes to conductivity, magnetization, and optical response on an equal footing. Much of what I do follows one pattern: identify a response that this geometry allows but conventional band theory misses, determine the symmetry conditions under which it survives, compute its magnitude in a concrete material, and formulate a measurement that can isolate it.

I work across three complementary formalisms, chosen to fit the problem. Semiclassical Boltzmann transport is tractable and physically transparent. Quantum kinetic density-matrix theory is necessary when interband coherence and scattering must be treated on the same footing. First-principles calculations supply quantitative, material-specific predictions. A defining feature of my work is close collaboration with experimental groups; several of my theoretical predictions were designed around, and subsequently confirmed by, transport measurements.

Systems of interest: Dirac and Weyl semimetals, Kramers-Weyl and spin-orbit coupled metals, multilayer and rhombohedral graphene, transition metal dichalcogenide homo- and heterostructures, moiré superlattices, and altermagnetic and antiferromagnetic materials.

Completed Research Themes

My published work organizes into six connected themes.

Theme I

Chiral anomalies and nonlinear magnetotransport in Weyl systems

The chiral anomaly, the non-conservation of chiral charge and energy between Weyl nodes, is one of the most striking manifestations of band topology in transport. Working within the Boltzmann framework with Landau levels, we showed that inversion-symmetry-broken, tilted Weyl semimetals support a finite longitudinal nonlinear magnetoconductivity that vanishes in untilted systems, and that its intricate dependence on tilt orientation makes second-harmonic generation a practical probe of the tilt geometry itself. We then established that chiral anomalies are not exclusive to Weyl semimetals: 3D spin-orbit coupled (Kramers-Weyl) metals host electrical, thermal, and gravitational chiral anomalies in which chiral charge is pumped between two Fermi surfaces attached to a single node with opposite Berry curvature flux, with consequences for spin thermal transport such as the spin Nernst effect. In collaboration with experiment, we extended this to the correlated regime, showing that disorder-driven Kondo interactions in type-II Weyl WTe2 pin the Fermi level near the Weyl nodes and thereby enhance Berry-curvature-driven nonequilibrium responses, producing a zero-field spontaneous Hall effect and a quadratically-scaling second-harmonic Hall signal.

References: Phys. Rev. B 105, 235408 (2022); Phys. Rev. B 108, 045405 (2023); Mater. Today Quantum 10, 1000070 (2026).
Theme II

Hidden planar band geometry and Hall responses in layered materials

In strictly 2D systems the conventional planar Hall effect is forbidden, because the out-of-plane Berry curvature cannot couple to in-plane band velocity. We showed that quasi-2D materials nevertheless host a genuine 2D planar Hall effect, sourced by hidden planar components of the Berry curvature and orbital magnetic moment that arise from interlayer tunneling. We identified every planar band-geometric contribution, classified their crystalline symmetry restrictions, and demonstrated in gated bilayer graphene that the effect is additionally sensitive to Lifshitz transitions. Extending the same logic to out-of-plane fields, we found that the combined action of electric and magnetic fields in layered materials couples layer polarization to the orbital moment, generating a mixed layer-orbital quantum geometry in field-dressed Bloch states. This produces an intrinsic magnetoelectric Hall effect that is bilinear in E and B, scattering-time independent, survives inside the band gap, and can appear even in nonmagnetic systems without spin-orbit coupling, with rhombohedral pentalayer graphene as a concrete realization. Together these establish hidden planar and layer-resolved geometry as a measurable, and previously overlooked, resource in layered materials.

References: Phys. Rev. Lett. 134, 026301 (2025); arXiv:2604.20249.
Theme III

Odd-parity magnetoresponse as a probe of time-reversal symmetry breaking

Detecting intrinsic time-reversal symmetry (TRS) breaking is difficult in materials whose crystal symmetries forbid the anomalous Hall effect. Working jointly with experiment, we demonstrated a giant, gate-tunable, room-temperature odd-parity magnetoresistance in a bilayer graphene/Cr2Ge2Te6 heterostructure, and traced it to the coupling of out-of-plane Berry curvature and orbital magnetic moment to the applied field in a TRS-broken system. We subsequently developed the general theory. Onsager reciprocity forbids any longitudinal magnetoconductivity linear in B when TRS is intact, so a finite B-linear longitudinal response is a direct and symmetry-protected fingerprint of intrinsic TRS breaking. This gives experimentalists a clean transport diagnostic precisely where the anomalous Hall effect is unavailable.

References: Phys. Rev. Lett. 134, 106301 (2025); Phys. Rev. B 113, 035408 (2026).
Theme IV

Generalized Onsager quantization and aperiodic quantum oscillations

Quantum oscillations are the workhorse probe of Fermi surface geometry, and their interpretation rests on Onsager's semiclassical quantization rule, which predicts oscillations strictly periodic in 1/B and a linear Landau fan diagram. We showed that higher-order magnetic-susceptibility corrections to the free energy generalize this rule, producing a nonlinear Landau fan, a field-dependent oscillation frequency, and a field-dependent cyclotron mass. We worked this out for three-dimensional systems, deriving the B-dependent frequency and a generalized Lifshitz-Kosevich equation, and spelling out the conditions under which the effects become observable. In parallel with experiment, these signatures were observed at the LaVO3/KTaO3 and EuO/KTaO3 interfaces, small-Fermi-surface systems where susceptibility corrections are large, providing direct evidence for magnetic-field-induced Fermi surface expansion.

References: Adv. Funct. Mater. 2422986 (2025); arXiv:2403.03765.
Theme V

Quantum-geometric spin responses and energy-efficient spintronics

A recurring goal in spintronics is to move spin angular momentum without moving charge, avoiding Joule heating. We introduced the intrinsic nonlinear pure spin Hall effect, in which both linear and second-order charge Hall currents vanish while second-order spin transport survives. A full symmetry analysis identified the 39 magnetic point groups that permit it, and first-principles calculations predict a sizable effect in the Kramers-Weyl metal RhGe even at room temperature. On the optical side, we showed that light-induced nonlinear spin magnetization becomes the dominant magnetization channel in centrosymmetric materials, resolved its distinct quantum-geometric contributions, and predicted a helicity-dependent response in the antiferromagnet CuMnAs that reverses with light helicity. Turning to magnetic order itself, we found that collinear altermagnets, unlike conventional antiferromagnets, host unequal sublattice spin torques. These enable field-free, deterministic 180° Néel vector switching on picosecond timescales, even in centrosymmetric, weakly spin-orbit-coupled systems where the Néel spin-orbit torque mechanism fails.

References: New J. Phys. 27, 113503 (2025); Phys. Rev. B (2026); Phys. Rev. B 113, 155430 (2026).
Theme VI

Valley physics and strain-engineered gauge fields in graphene

Nonuniform strain in graphene acts as a valley-dependent gauge field, generating pseudomagnetic fields that mimic real magnetic fields while preserving global time-reversal symmetry. Although local probes had visualized such fields, quantitative detection through macroscopic transport remained elusive. Together with experiment, we showed that high-mobility graphene develops distinct beating patterns in Shubnikov-de Haas oscillations from valley-resolved Landau quantization under different effective fields, and that the universal scaling of the node carrier density and filling factor with applied field allows pseudomagnetic fields as small as a few millitesla to be extracted. We have since generalized this into a diagnostic framework. Because strain, valley imbalance, valley-dependent energy shifts, spin-orbit splitting, and Kekulé distortion each produce a distinct scaling of the beating nodes with carrier density and field, the beating pattern itself identifies which mechanism is at play. I also contributed to a community Roadmap surveying the state and open challenges of 2D valleytronics, spanning valley Hall effects, lightwave valleytronics, flat-band valleytronics, and spin-valley qubits.

References: Phys. Rev. Lett. 136, 166604 (2026); arXiv:2606.20339; arXiv:2603.01427.

Ongoing and Future Directions

Where I intend to take this work next, building on the themes above and increasingly aimed at turning quantum geometry from a descriptive language into a predictive, design-oriented one.

Direction 1

Higher-order quantum geometry, nonlinear transport and optics

Most quantum-geometric transport theory rests on two objects, the Berry curvature and the quantum metric, defined for charge in a single-band-index sense. My recent work on mixed layer-orbital geometry and hidden planar geometry suggests these are special cases of a much richer structure. Whenever an internal degree of freedom (layer, sublattice, valley, spin, orbital) can be addressed by an external field, a corresponding resolved geometric tensor appears and sources its own response. Higher-order objects such as the Berry connection polarizability enter at second order in the field and control intrinsic nonlinear conductivity, second-harmonic generation, and photocurrents. I want to develop this systematically and apply it to multilayer and moiré platforms, including rhombohedral graphene, twisted bilayers, and TMD heterostructures, where the internal degrees of freedom are both numerous and experimentally tunable by gating, twist angle, and displacement field. The aim is a unified account of which geometric object controls which response, and how to switch between them in situ.

Direction 2

Correlated quantum geometry and superconducting transport

Quantum geometry does not switch off when interactions dominate; it reorganizes. In flat-band and moiré systems the superfluid stiffness has an irreducibly geometric contribution, so superconductivity there is controlled by wavefunction structure rather than by dispersion. I want to extend the machinery I have developed for normal-state responses into this regime, asking how the geometric contribution to Josephson coupling, critical current, and nonreciprocal (diode-like) superconducting transport can be computed and, more importantly, distinguished experimentally from conventional mechanisms. The same symmetry logic that governs which normal-state responses are allowed should constrain which superconducting diode and nonlinear responses can appear, and this connection seems largely unexploited.

Direction 3

Spin, orbital, and altermagnetic responses

Charge is only one of the currents a crystal can carry, and spin and orbital angular momentum transport are what matter for low-dissipation device functionality. Building on the nonlinear pure spin Hall effect and the asymmetric sublattice torque mechanism, I want to push toward orbital analogues of these responses, which are far less understood than their spin counterparts, and toward altermagnets specifically. Altermagnets combine nonrelativistic spin splitting with a vanishing net moment, which makes their transport signatures unusually sensitive to symmetry and unusually attractive for memory applications. My goal is to identify measurable transport fingerprints of altermagnetic order and realistic switching protocols, stated in quantities an experimentalist can access directly.

Methods thread

Foundations: scattering, collision integrals, and the intrinsic-extrinsic divide

Cutting across all three directions is a formal question that is currently unsettled. Intrinsic nonlinear responses sourced by the quantum metric are among the most actively studied quantities in quantum transport, and also among the most disputed. The disagreement is not about the geometry but about the kinetics. Different treatments of the equilibrium density matrix and the collision integral in quantum kinetic theory yield different, sometimes unphysical, answers, including dissipative contributions that should not exist. I am working out explicitly how the equilibrium distribution and the scattering term must be defined consistently with one another, so that field-dressed energies and disorder are treated on the same footing. Settling this matters well beyond the formalism, since it determines whether a measured nonlinear signal can legitimately be read as a quantum-geometric quantity, which is precisely what a growing body of experiments assumes.
