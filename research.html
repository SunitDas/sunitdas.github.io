Research Interests

I'm mostly interested in new transport and optical effects caused by quantum geometry in topological systems like Dirac and Weyl semimetals. I'm also interested in the transport and optical properties of different two-dimensional systems, especially moiré systems like twisted bilayer graphene, and homo- and hetero-structures of transition metal dichalcogenides (TMDs). While these broadly define my current research interests, I'm also happy to explore other stimulating topics in condensed matter physics.

We use different formalisms depending on the specific problem at hand — most frequently the semiclassical Boltzmann transport formalism, which is not only tractable but also the most physically intuitive, alongside quantum kinetic (density-matrix) approaches when interband coherence effects matter, and first-principles calculations for material-specific predictions. Much of this work is done in close collaboration with experimental groups.

Research Directions

My published work organizes into six connected themes. References in brackets point to the corresponding entries on the Publications page.

Direction I

Chiral anomalies and nonlinear magnetotransport in Weyl systems

The chiral anomaly — the non-conservation of chiral charge and energy between Weyl nodes — is one of the most striking manifestations of band topology in transport. Working within the Boltzmann framework with Landau levels, we showed that inversion-symmetry-broken, tilted Weyl semimetals support a finite longitudinal nonlinear magnetoconductivity that vanishes in untilted systems, and that its intricate dependence on tilt orientation makes second-harmonic generation a practical probe of the tilt geometry itself [1]. We then established that chiral anomalies are not exclusive to Weyl semimetals: 3D spin-orbit coupled (Kramers-Weyl) metals host electrical, thermal, and gravitational chiral anomalies in which the chiral charge is pumped between two Fermi surfaces attached to a single node with opposite Berry curvature flux, with consequences for spin thermal transport such as the spin Nernst effect [2]. In collaboration with experiment, we extended this to the correlated regime, showing that disorder-driven Kondo interactions in type-II Weyl WTe2 pin the Fermi level near the Weyl nodes and thereby enhance Berry-curvature-driven nonequilibrium responses — a zero-field spontaneous Hall effect and a quadratically-scaling second-harmonic Hall signal [11].

Direction II

Hidden planar band geometry and Hall responses in layered materials

In strictly 2D systems the conventional planar Hall effect is forbidden, because the out-of-plane Berry curvature cannot couple to in-plane band velocity. We showed that quasi-2D materials nevertheless host a genuine 2D planar Hall effect, sourced by hidden planar components of the Berry curvature and orbital magnetic moment that arise from interlayer tunneling. We identified every planar band-geometric contribution, classified their crystalline symmetry restrictions, and demonstrated in gated bilayer graphene that the effect is additionally sensitive to Lifshitz transitions [3]. Extending the same logic to out-of-plane fields, we found that the combined action of electric and magnetic fields in layered materials couples layer polarization to the orbital moment, generating a mixed layer-orbital quantum geometry in field-dressed Bloch states. This produces an intrinsic magnetoelectric Hall effect that is bilinear in E and B, scattering-time independent, survives inside the band gap, and can appear even in nonmagnetic systems without spin-orbit coupling — with rhombohedral pentalayer graphene as a concrete realization [14]. Together these establish hidden planar and layer-resolved geometry as a measurable, and previously overlooked, resource in layered materials.

Direction III

Odd-parity magnetoresponse as a probe of time-reversal symmetry breaking

Detecting intrinsic time-reversal symmetry (TRS) breaking is difficult in materials whose crystal symmetries forbid the anomalous Hall effect. Working jointly with experiment, we demonstrated a giant, gate-tunable, room-temperature odd-parity magnetoresistance in a bilayer graphene/Cr2Ge2Te6 heterostructure, and traced it to the coupling of out-of-plane Berry curvature and orbital magnetic moment to the applied field in a TRS-broken system [4]. We subsequently developed the general theory: Onsager reciprocity forbids any longitudinal magnetoconductivity linear in B when TRS is intact, so a finite B-linear longitudinal response is a direct and symmetry-protected fingerprint of intrinsic TRS breaking [7]. This gives experimentalists a clean transport diagnostic precisely where the anomalous Hall effect is unavailable.

Direction IV

Generalized Onsager quantization and aperiodic quantum oscillations

Quantum oscillations are the workhorse probe of Fermi surface geometry, and their interpretation rests on Onsager's semiclassical quantization rule, which predicts oscillations strictly periodic in 1/B and a linear Landau fan diagram. We showed that higher-order magnetic-susceptibility corrections to the free energy generalize this rule, producing a nonlinear Landau fan, a field-dependent oscillation frequency, and a field-dependent cyclotron mass. We worked this out for three-dimensional systems, deriving the B-dependent frequency and a generalized Lifshitz–Kosevich equation, and spelling out the conditions under which the effects become observable [12]. In parallel with experiment, these signatures were observed at the LaVO3–KTaO3 and EuO–KTaO3 interfaces — small-Fermi-surface systems where susceptibility corrections are large — providing direct evidence for magnetic-field-induced Fermi surface expansion [5].

Direction V

Quantum-geometric spin responses and energy-efficient spintronics

A recurring goal in spintronics is to move spin angular momentum without moving charge, avoiding Joule heating. We introduced the intrinsic nonlinear pure spin Hall effect, in which both linear and second-order charge Hall currents vanish while second-order spin transport survives; a full symmetry analysis identified the 39 magnetic point groups that permit it, and first-principles calculations predict a sizable effect in the Kramers-Weyl metal RhGe even at room temperature [8]. On the optical side, we showed that light-induced nonlinear spin magnetization becomes the dominant magnetization channel in centrosymmetric materials, resolved its distinct quantum-geometric contributions, and predicted a helicity-dependent response in the antiferromagnet CuMnAs that reverses with light helicity [6]. Turning to magnetic order itself, we found that collinear altermagnets — unlike conventional antiferromagnets — host unequal sublattice spin torques, enabling field-free, deterministic 180° Néel vector switching on picosecond timescales even in centrosymmetric, weakly spin-orbit-coupled systems where the Néel spin-orbit torque mechanism fails [9].

Direction VI

Valley physics and strain-engineered gauge fields in graphene

Nonuniform strain in graphene acts as a valley-dependent gauge field, generating pseudomagnetic fields that mimic real magnetic fields while preserving global time-reversal symmetry. Although local probes had visualized such fields, quantitative detection through macroscopic transport remained elusive. Together with experiment, we showed that high-mobility graphene develops distinct beating patterns in Shubnikov–de Haas oscillations from valley-resolved Landau quantization under different effective fields, and that the universal scaling of the node carrier density and filling factor with applied field allows pseudomagnetic fields as small as a few millitesla to be extracted [10]. This establishes quantum oscillation spectroscopy as a broadly applicable probe of strain-induced gauge fields in Dirac materials. I also contributed to a community Roadmap surveying the state and open challenges of 2D valleytronics, spanning valley Hall effects, lightwave valleytronics, flat-band valleytronics, and spin-valley qubits [13].

Ongoing Work

Directions currently in progress, described at an abstract level.

A constructive symmetry classification of response tensors

Existing tools answer "given a symmetry group, what tensor form does a response take?" This project inverts the question: given a response (linear or nonlinear, of any tensor rank), which of the 122 magnetic point groups permit it, component by component? The approach reduces each response tensor to its underlying multipole content, builds a bridge between the tensor-symmetry, multipole-classification, and quantum-geometry languages that the literature currently treats separately, and aims to turn "symmetry says this is allowed" into "here is the mechanism and the material class to target."

Nonlocal resistance networks and valley Hall transport

Nonlocal resistance measurements are a standard probe for valley currents in graphene devices, but the microscopic origin of the signal remains debated. This project develops and compares an edge-channel resistor-network model against a bulk valley drift-diffusion model to explain the exponential decay of the nonlocal signal with distance and its scaling with resistivity, clarifying which mechanism dominates in a given regime.

Consistency of quantum-metric-induced nonlinear Hall transport

Intrinsic nonlinear Hall responses driven by the quantum metric are computed using quantum kinetic (density-matrix) theory, but different treatments of the equilibrium distribution and the collision term can produce spurious dissipative contributions. This project works out explicitly how the equilibrium distribution and the collision integral must be defined consistently with one another — contributing to an actively-debated question in the quantum-transport community about isolating the purely intrinsic part of the response.
